Block 1 of 8

Food for an agent

An AI agent thinks by running a model, and running a model costs somebody a GPU-second. So an agent that wants to think needs a way to pay for compute.

That is the whole idea. $FLOP is the thing it pays with.

Block 2 of 8

Mining that makes something

Bitcoin miners burn electricity on puzzles that mean nothing outside the game. Flop wants the same security budget spent on work a customer actually asked for.

Old wayGuess numbers until one fits. The answer is useless to everyone.
Flop’s wayRun the model an agent asked for. The answer is the product.

The name for it: proof-of-useful-inference. The door is deliberately wide — the stated minimum is one GPU with 16 GB of VRAM, not a data centre.

Block 3 of 8

How anyone knows the work was done

Here is the hard part. You paid a stranger to think for you. They send back an answer. How do you know they ran the model at all — rather than a cheaper one, a cached reply, or nothing?

Flop stacks four machine checks. No humans anywhere in the loop.

The chip vouchesA trusted execution environment signs off that the run really happened on real hardware.
Show your workingTOPLOC — a fingerprint of the model’s own internals during the run.
Spot-check itValidators re-run a sample from scratch and compare.
Money on the tableMiners stake $FLOP. Caught cheating, they can lose all of it.

The first three make cheating detectable. The fourth makes it expensive — which is the one that actually changes behaviour.

Block 4 of 8

Who gets paid, and how much

Every inference has a fee, and it splits two ways. Almost all of it goes to the machine that did the thinking.

85% MINERS15%
Miners ran the model · 85%Validators checked it · 15%

The teaser says the payment is liquid straight away — no lockup, no vesting.

Block 5 of 8

The checkers, and how they lose the job

Validators do the checking, and there is a hard ceiling: 1,000 seats, however many want in.

Every month the bottom 50 are thrown out and replaced by the best candidates waiting — ranked on uptime, accuracy and latency. The seat is rented by performance, not owned.

1,000 validators, capped bottom 50 replaced monthly rule changes need 2 in every 3

Rule changes are proposed as FIPs — Flop Improvement Protocols — and need two thirds of the validator set to pass.

Block 6 of 8

Where the tokens go

The teaser projects 17.2 billion $FLOP in existence by year ten. The split says plainly who the network is built for.

Miners51.2% Airdrop20.4% Team & foundation11.4% Validators6.8% Brokers & agents6.8% Staking rewards3.4%

Miners take more than everything else combined. The team and foundation take 11.4% between them, and stop being paid after year ten.

Block 7 of 8

The order of things

Now read these two dates in order, because the order is the surprising part.

  • Q4 2026 · testnetA practice network opens. What you do on it decides your slice of the 3.5 billion $FLOP airdrop — earned, not claimed.
  • … then the airdropTokens are handed out here — while the real chain still does not exist.
  • Q1 2027 · mainnetThe first real block. Roughly one second per block, 96 $FLOP each, halving every 730 days for five halvings.

For a few months, holders have a claim on a chain that has not started. Unusual is not the same as wrong — but it is worth noticing yourself rather than being told later.

Block 8 of 8

How solid is any of this

Flop Labs is run by Arthur Hayes, co-founder of BitMEX. The launch terms are blunt: no pre-sale, no VCs, 100% fair launch.

And every number in the seven blocks above comes from a document that calls itself version 0.1, draft, and says in its own words that the figures are provisional and the Yellow Paper is not final.

provisional by its own admission not launched nothing here is advice

A project that tells you which of its numbers might move is doing you a favour. Repeating them as settled fact is how a draft becomes a rumour.
